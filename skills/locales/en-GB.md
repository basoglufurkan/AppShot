# English (United Kingdom)

Not "en-US with a u in colour". British readers notice American hype and vocabulary immediately and discount the app for it. Rewrite; don't spell-check.

## Voice and register

- **You**, direct, with contractions, as in the US, but **turned down one notch**. Understatement and dry wit land; breathless enthusiasm reads as American marketing. "Properly good" persuades more than "Awesome!".
- Confidence through calm specifics: "Bills sorted before payday" beats "Crush your finances".
- **Light colloquialisms** ("sorted", "proper", "a doddle", "faff") suit friendly consumer brands. Use them once per set at most, and never in finance or health, where plain and reassuring wins (think GOV.UK clarity).
- **Avoid aggressive verbs** imported from US fitness and hustle culture: crush, dominate, slay, hustle, grind.

## Typography rules

- **British spelling:** organise, personalise, colour, favourite, centre, travelling, grey, programme (TV/plan) but "program" (software), cheque, tyre, mum, maths, licence (noun) / license (verb).
- **Sentence case is strongly preferred.** UK publishing and public-sector style use it; Title Case looks imported.
- **Quotes:** single quotes ‘ ’ are the common UK convention, with double “ ” nested. Pick one and apply it everywhere.
- **Dashes:** a spaced en dash ( – ) rather than an unspaced em dash.
- **Money and dates:** "£4.99" (VAT-inclusive prices are the norm, but avoid prices in captions). Dates are "4 November" (day before month); times "9.30pm" or "21:30".
- **No full stops** in abbreviations or titles ("Mr", "Dr", "eg" is acceptable in informal brands). No exclamation marks on the hook.

## Length

- Same as en-US. UK synonyms are sometimes longer ("mobile phone" vs "phone") and sometimes shorter ("flat" vs "apartment"). Pick the shorter native word.
- Keep headlines to 3–5 words. A UK headline with two jokes is one joke too many.

## Store and search habits

- **Search vocabulary differs from the US:** "budget planner", "money manager", "food diary", "calorie counter", "takeaway", "train times", "football scores", "revision app" (not "study app"), "council tax", "direct debit", "bin collection", "MOT reminder". Echo the UK term in captions; it also helps OCR-indexed text match UK queries.
- **The UK storefront shows en-GB metadata when provided.** Without it, the store falls back to your primary language, usually en-US. Localising captions is cheap differentiation, because many competitors never do it.
- **Subscription scepticism is high.** "No ads" and "free to start" must match the actual product. Trial and cancellation terms belong on the paywall, not in screenshots. The ASA (Advertising Standards Authority, CAP Code) is active on misleading "free" and comparative claims.
- **Payment and banking context:** Open Banking, Monzo- and Starling-style instant notifications and "pots" are familiar concepts. Budget apps can borrow that vocabulary if the feature exists.

## Transcreation examples

| US headline | UK headline | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Get organized | Get **organised** | spelling |
| Cut your takeout spending | Spend less on **takeaways** | vocabulary |
| Awesome workouts, anywhere | Proper **workouts**, anywhere | "awesome" reads as American hype |
| Your vacation, planned | Your **holiday**, sorted | vocabulary + UK colloquial "sorted" |
| Crush your goals | Hit your **goals** | tone down the aggression |
| Save on groceries | Spend less on the **big shop** | UK idiom for the weekly grocery run |
| Math homework, solved | **Maths** homework, sorted | "maths" is non-negotiable |
| Never miss a bill | Never miss a **direct debit** | the UK's default way to pay bills |
| Find an apartment fast | Find a **flat**, fast | vocabulary |
| Live soccer scores | Live **football** scores | "soccer" signals a US app |
| Trash the clutter | **Bin** the clutter | vocabulary, and shorter |
| Text your friends | **Message** your mates | "mates" for casual brands only |

## Pitfalls

- **Unchanged Americanisms:** cell, apartment, vacation, fall, sneakers, check (bill), zip code, ER, gas (fuel). Each one tells the reader the app wasn't made for them.
- **Over-Britishing** ("Cheerio!", "innit", "Blimey") is a caricature. Write the way a modern UK brand writes.
- **Words that change meaning:** "pants" means underwear; "quite good" means mediocre (understatement); "pissed" means drunk; "fanny" is vulgar. Avoid "brilliant" as an empty intensifier.
- **The NHS name and logo** cannot be used to imply endorsement. Health apps should not suggest NHS approval unless they hold it.
- **ASA rules** require that "free" and comparative claims ("cheaper than", "UK's favourite") are true and substantiated. "UK's No.1" needs current, documented evidence.
