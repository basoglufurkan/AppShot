# English (United States)

Usually the source locale and the most competitive storefront. Every top competitor has a polished set here, so generic copy disappears. Specific, concrete benefits win.

## Voice and register

- Address the reader as **you**, directly, with contractions ("you'll", "don't"). There is no formal/informal split. Register comes from word choice: "Get your evenings back" is casual; "Reclaim your evenings" is premium.
- **Imperatives and outcome statements** dominate: "Sleep deeper", "Know where every dollar goes".
- US audiences tolerate energy and confidence, but they have seen every superlative. Confidence should come from specifics ("Bills paid on time") rather than adjectives ("Amazing budgeting").
- **Avoid "we" in headlines.** Brand voice can appear in a trust-shot subhead ("We never sell your data").
- **Category cues.** Fitness and productivity can be punchy and a little brash ("Crush leg day"). Finance and health should sound calm and competent. Kids and education: talk to the parent, not the child.

## Typography rules

- **Spelling:** American (color, organize, favorite, center, traveling, gray, catalog). Fix any -ise/-our that slips in from a shared English source.
- **Case:** sentence case is the modern default ("Plan your week in minutes"). Title Case reads more ad-like. Pick one for the whole set; never mix.
- **Ending punctuation:** none on headlines. A deliberate period works only as a stylistic choice for the *whole* set ("Budgeting. Done."). Never use exclamation marks on the hook.
- **Quotes and dashes:** curly “ ” and ’, never straight quotes in display text. Em dash without spaces ("Fast—and free") in subheads; avoid dashes in headlines.
- **Numerals:** use digits in headlines ("3 taps", "10 minutes"), not words. Write `$` before the amount ("$4.99"); dates as "Nov 4"; times as "9:30 PM" in mock UI.
- **Oxford comma** in subheads that list three items.

## Length

- This is the baseline: other locales expand from here, so leave slack in the layout.
- Aim for 3–5 words in the headline and ≤ 24 characters per line. If English doesn't fit two lines, the translation won't fit three.
- Cut articles and auxiliaries ("Your budget is now automatic" → "Budgets that build themselves"). Prefer one strong verb over verb + adverb ("quickly find" → "find").

## Store and search habits

- **US queries are short and functional:** "budget app", "habit tracker", "calorie counter", "white noise", "photo editor", "pdf scanner", "ai chat", "meal planner". People add "free" and "app" to queries more than they type a brand name. Title, subtitle and keyword field carry the ranking; captions should *echo* the main term once so the searcher sees they landed in the right place.
- **Subscriptions** are normalized, and so is suspicion of them. "Cancel anytime" and transparent terms convert, but on the paywall. Screenshots sell the outcome; trial and price wording stays out of them.
- **Competitor style:** top US apps (Calm, Duolingo, Strava, YNAB-style budgeting, Notion-style productivity) use one idea per shot, large sentence-case type, little UI chrome and a social-proof shot late in the set. Scan the top 5 results for the target keyword before writing, then differentiate rather than copy.
- **Proof expectations:** awards like "App of the Day" or "Editors' Choice" work only when real and exactly worded. Rating shots work at {rating} ≥ 4.5 with a large {ratings_count}.

## Transcreation examples

English is the source here, so the table shows the native rewrite of drafts that sound like feature lists or translations.

| Draft | US-native headline | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Expense tracking feature | Know where every **dollar** goes | feature → outcome; "dollar" localizes "money" |
| Advanced sleep analysis | Wake up **rested** | the user buys the morning, not the analysis |
| Home workout programs | Get **strong** at home | verb + outcome beats a noun list |
| Smart budgeting tool | A budget you'll actually **keep** | "smart" is filler; promise adherence |
| Efficient language learning | Speak **Spanish** sooner | name the language the user searched |
| Bill reminder notifications | No more **late fees** | name the pain, not the mechanism |
| Professional photo filters | Edit like a **pro** | short, familiar, translatable |
| Collaborative grocery lists | One list for the whole **house** | shared use made concrete |
| Meal planning made simple | Dinner, **decided** | "made simple" is the most overused phrase in the store |
| Focus timer with statistics | Get your **focus** back | emotional payoff first; stats can go in a subhead |
| Seamless cloud sync | Pick up on any **device** | "seamless" means nothing at thumbnail size |
| Privacy-first architecture | Your data stays **yours** | architecture is a proof point, not a hook |

## Pitfalls

- **British spellings and vocabulary** from a shared English file: "organise", "colour", "mobile" instead of "phone", "holiday" instead of "vacation", "takeaway" instead of "takeout".
- **Superlatives and "#1" without substantiation.** FTC rules on deceptive advertising and the 2024 FTC rule against fake reviews and testimonials apply. "Best", "#1" and "clinically proven" need evidence you can produce.
- **Health and diet claims** ("Lose 10 lbs in 2 weeks") trigger both FTC scrutiny and store review. Promise habits and tracking, not medical outcomes.
- **"Free" on an app with a paywall** reads as bait, and trial terms don't belong in screenshots at all. Say "Free to start" only if a real free tier exists; trial details live on the paywall.
- **Over-casual slang** ("lit", "no cap", "slay") dates within months and alienates anyone over 30. Use it only for apps built for Gen Z, and only if the UI talks the same way.
- **Sports and holiday references** (Super Bowl, Thanksgiving) are fine for custom product pages and seasonal sets, never for the evergreen set.
