# Home Workout

## The searcher

- A busy person (often a parent or office worker) who wants to move without a gym membership or commute. They search "home workout no equipment".
- A beginner embarrassed to go to a gym who wants low-impact, follow-along sessions in their living room.
- Someone motivated by weight loss who wants a structured challenge with a clear start and finish.
- A trend-follower who saw "wall pilates" or a "28-day challenge" on social media.
- What they want to feel: "Short enough to actually do today, and I just have to follow along."

## What wins the install

1. **Time and place promise.** "{minutes} minutes, no equipment, at home" kills the two big objections, time and access, in one line.
2. **Follow-along guidance.** An animated or video trainer plus a countdown means no thinking and no planning.
3. **Challenge structure.** 28- and 30-day plans give motivation, progress and a finish line.
4. **Beginner-safe.** Levels, low-impact options and modifications reassure people who feel unfit.
5. **Personal plan.** A quiz result ("Your plan: {weeks} weeks, Toning, Beginner") makes the plan feel tailored and is the conversion moment for leaders.
6. **Streaks and progress.** Visible consistency keeps the habit alive.

## Visual direction

- Warm and energetic but approachable: ember and sunset gradients, or playful for a casual, fun brand. Friendlier than gym apps.
- Trainers in real home settings (living room, yoga mat, sofa nearby) with relatable bodies and ages. Women-focused palettes and models if that's the target.
- Typography: rounded bold sans with large timers and day numbers.
- Leaders use trainer cutouts popping out of the device, bright gradient backgrounds, challenge calendars and quiz screens. Keep the home context visible. A gym backdrop contradicts the promise.

## Avoid

- "Lose belly fat" / spot-reduction claims, and "lose 5 kg in 2 weeks" promises. They're misleading health claims under Apple 1.4.1 / 2.3.1 and Google Play's health misinformation policy. Promise habit, energy and strength instead.
- Before/after body photos, tape measures around waists, or scales showing dramatic drops.
- Calorie burn numbers presented as exact. Label them as estimates.
- Pregnancy, postpartum or injury programs without in-app guidance to consult a professional.
- Fabricated transformations, testimonials or "{users} women joined" counts.
- Prices, trial offers or "limited time" text in screenshots.
- Conversion killers: gym stock photos (they contradict "at home"), a 45-minute session as the hero, and text-heavy feature lists before the user has seen a single workout.
