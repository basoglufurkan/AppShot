# Language Learning

## The searcher

- Has a deadline with a face on it: a trip in a few weeks, a job interview, a move abroad, a partner's family they want to talk to. They want to *speak*, not collect points.
- The burned-out restarter: kept a long streak in another app and still freezes when a waiter asks a question. Skeptical of gamification, hungry for real conversation.
- The exam taker (IELTS, TOEFL) with a target score and a date — wants structure and measurable progress.
- The commuter who will give it 5–10 minutes a day if it never feels like homework.
- After installing they want to feel: "I said something real in {language} today."

## What wins the install

1. **Speaking from day one** — the #1 objection is "apps don't make you talk"; show a real exchange in the target language, not a multiple-choice quiz.
2. **Tiny daily commitment** — "{minutes} minutes a day" lowers the cost of starting; pair it with a visible lesson-length chip.
3. **AI conversation partner** — role-play with instant correction is the current reason people switch apps, and it only converts when the correction is visible.
4. **Starts at your level** — a placement test or adaptive path kills the "too easy, I'm not a beginner" bounce from intermediates.
5. **Pronunciation feedback** — a score or waveform makes an invisible skill tangible.
6. **Proof of progress** — words learned, CEFR level, streak: numbers that belong to the user.
7. **Real-life scenarios** — airport, café, interview, doctor: concrete moments beat abstract "units".

## Story (shot by shot)

1. **Hook** — you're already speaking — show: AI conversation / role-play with one inline correction — layout: chat-bubbles — angle: target-language bubbles oversized and floating out of the device, caption in the store language.
2. **Habit** — small enough to start today — show: a lesson in progress with its length chip — layout: hero-device — angle: "{minutes} minutes a day".
3. **Pronunciation** — hear yourself improve — show: speaking exercise with score/waveform — layout: zoom-callout — angle: zoom on the score and the corrected syllable.
4. **Your level** — no repeating the basics — show: placement result or course path — layout: device-top — angle: "start at B1, not at hello".
5. **Real life** — learn what you'll actually say — show: scenario list (travel, work, dating, doctor) — layout: floating-cards — angle: 3–4 scenario cards orbiting the device.
6. **Progress** — proof it works — show: stats screen (words, streak, level) — layout: big-number — angle: one hero metric from real data, e.g. {words} words.
7. **Choice** — the languages on offer — show: language picker — layout: feature-list — angle: the 3 most-searched languages named, the rest as "+{languageCount}".
8. **Trust** (optional) — show: verified rating or editorial feature — layout: social-proof — angle: only with a real {rating} or award.

## Supporting elements

- **AI tutor chat**: tutor bubble in the target language ("¿Qué te gustaría pedir?"), the user's reply, and a small green correction chip under it ("Better: Quisiera un café"). Keep it short, correct and checked by a native speaker.
- **Streak notification**: "{streak}-day streak — 5 minutes keeps it alive".
- **Stat cards**: "{words} words learned", "Level {cefr}", "{minutes} min today".
- **Pronunciation pill**: "{score}% match" beside a waveform.
- **Lesson chip**: "5 min · Airport check-in".
- **Scenario cards**: Travel / Work / Dating / Doctor, each with a one-line example sentence.
- **Comparison row**: "Memorize word lists" vs "Speak from lesson one" — only if the app really starts with speaking.
- **Language strip**: "{languageCount} languages" with names, not just flags.

## Visual direction

- Bright, optimistic, friendly: saturated green/orange/sky with lots of white; rounded geometric sans (Nunito, Poppins, SF Rounded) in heavy weights.
- Two personalities top the charts: mascot-led playful (bold colour, characters breaking the frame) and adult/professional (clean-light, calm blue, photos of real situations). Exam and career apps go clean; casual apps go playful.
- AI-tutor apps lean dark with one vivid gradient accent and oversized chat bubbles overlapping the device edge.
- Target-language text inside the UI, captions in the storefront language; never three languages on one shot.
- Use flags sparingly (they are nationality symbols, not languages); language names read better at thumbnail size.
- Keep exercise text large — crop with device-top or zoom-callout rather than showing a dense full screen.

## Avoid

- Outcome guarantees — "Fluent in 30 days", "Guaranteed IELTS 7": unsubstantiated performance claims break Apple 2.3.1 (accurate metadata) and consumer-protection rules. Say "prepare for", "practise for".
- Competitor names in captions or comparison rows ("Better than Duolingo") — Apple 2.3.7 bars other apps' names in metadata, and it's a trademark risk on Google Play too.
- Exam trademarks implying endorsement: IELTS, TOEFL belong to their owners; write "for IELTS prep", never "official".
- Fake learner counts, "#1 language app", or awards the app hasn't received.
- Prices, "free trial" banners or paywall screens inside screenshots (2.3.7).
- Wrong or unnatural target-language content — native speakers read your screenshots and say so in reviews.
- Conversion killers: opening with a course map or settings, tiny multiple-choice text, a wall of 30 flags, a mascot with no product visible, a microphone-permission dialog as shot 1.
