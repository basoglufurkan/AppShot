# Health Vitals Log

## The searcher

- Someone told by a doctor to monitor blood pressure or blood sugar at home who wants an easy log instead of paper. They search "blood pressure tracker".
- A person managing hypertension or diabetes who wants trends and a report to bring to appointments.
- A wellness-minded user tracking resting heart rate or HRV alongside a wearable.
- An adult child tracking a parent's readings.
- What they want to feel: "I understand my numbers, and I walk into my doctor's office with a clear report."

## What wins the install

1. **Readable trends.** Numbers over time become understanding. A clean chart with averages is the hero.
2. **Effortless logging.** Large entry controls, or sync from connected certified devices, remove the chore.
3. **Doctor-ready reports.** A PDF or shareable summary is a concrete, practical outcome people search for.
4. **Context.** Tags (after coffee, stressed, medication taken) and reminders make the data meaningful.
5. **Platform sync.** Apple Health / Health Connect integration signals the app fits their ecosystem.
6. **Transparency.** Clear sources for ranges and an honest methodology build trust and keep you within policy.

## Supporting elements

- Reading card: "{systolic}/{diastolic} mmHg · {pulse} bpm", with time and tags.
- Trend line chart with weekly average band and morning/evening split.
- Range legend that names its source in-app (for example a national cardiology guideline).
- PDF report preview card: date range, averages, readings table.
- Reminder notification: "Time for your evening reading".
- Sync badges: official Apple Health / Health Connect artwork per their guidelines.
- Tag chips: After coffee, Stressed, Medication taken, After exercise.

## Visual direction

- Clean-light, sky, soft-gray and mint: clinical trust with warmth, and strong contrast.
- Big tabular numerals with clear units. Charts large, with sensible axis ranges and plain labels.
- Use neutral or soft color coding. Avoid alarming red states in the hero, which create anxiety and imply diagnosis.
- Generic, unbranded cuff and glucometer photos if devices appear. Leaders use white/blue dashboards, big readings and report exports.

## Avoid

- Apple 1.4.1: apps claiming to measure blood pressure, blood glucose, body temperature or blood oxygen, or take x-rays, using only the phone's sensors are not permitted. Never imply the phone measures these. Show manual entry or connected certified devices.
- Camera-based heart rate: disclose methodology and accuracy in-app, state "not a medical device", and keep claims modest.
- Diagnostic claims ("detects hypertension", "AFib detection", "diabetes risk score"). That's medical-device territory (FDA, EU MDR), and Google Play's health policy requires an accurate Health apps declaration. Medical claims need evidence and may trigger review.
- Treatment advice based on readings ("adjust your medication"). Apple 1.4.1 also asks apps to remind users to consult a doctor.
- Real patient data. Use plausible demo readings, not dramatic crisis values.
- Device brand logos (cuff and meter manufacturers) without a partnership.
- Prices, trials, "clinically validated" badges without proof, or invented ratings.
- Conversion killers: tiny numbers, alarming red dashboards, and a feature soup instead of one clear trend story.
