# Medication Reminder

## The searcher

- Someone starting a new medication routine (or juggling several) who is afraid of forgetting or double-dosing. They search "pill reminder".
- A caregiver managing medications for an elderly parent, a partner or a child, often remotely.
- An older adult who needs large, clear, reliable reminders and a simple interface.
- Someone with a long-term condition who wants a dose history to show their doctor.
- What they want to feel: "I don't have to keep it all in my head. It's handled."

## What wins the install

1. **Reminders that act.** A notification with Taken / Snooze buttons is the core proof, and the job people hire the app for.
2. **A clear daily schedule.** Morning, noon and evening, with big type. Readability signals care, especially for older users.
3. **Caregiver peace of mind.** Alerts to family when a dose is missed (if the feature exists) widen the buyer to adult children.
4. **Refill tracking.** "You have {pillsLeft} left" prevents running out, a real-world pain.
5. **Doctor-ready history.** A shareable report turns logging into something useful at appointments.
6. **Simple setup.** Adding a medication in a few taps kills "this will be complicated".

## Story (shot by shot)

1. **Hook** — handled — show: today's schedule with doses by time of day and taken checkmarks — layout: hero-device — angle: your meds, on schedule.
2. **Reminders** — the core job — show: notifications with "Time for {medName} {dose}" and Taken / Snooze actions — layout: notification-stack — angle: reminders you'll notice.
3. **Refills** — prevention — show: pill inventory card with pills left and a refill reminder — layout: floating-cards — angle: refills before you run out.
4. **Caregiver** — family — show: the patient's phone and the caregiver's phone with a missed-dose alert — layout: duo-devices — angle: care for family, even from afar.
5. **History** — appointments — show: adherence calendar and a PDF report preview pulled out — layout: ui-pieces — angle: share a clear report with your doctor.
6. **Ease** — close — show: quick add, large text mode, vitamins too, data privacy (only true claims) — layout: feature-list — angle: simple for everyone in the family.

## Supporting elements

- Notification: "Time for {medName} {dose}" with Taken / Snooze / Skip action buttons.
- Dose timeline: Morning 08:00 (taken), Noon 13:00 (taken), Evening 20:00 (upcoming).
- Pill inventory card: "{pillsLeft} left · refill by {refillDate}".
- Caregiver alert: "{name} hasn't logged the evening dose".
- Adherence calendar with taken days filled and a monthly rate "{rate}%".
- Doctor report PDF preview with a date range and dose table.
- Generic pill shape and color icons (round white, capsule blue) instead of real packaging.

## Visual direction

- Clean-light, sky, mint and soft-gray: calm, clinical-but-friendly, trustworthy.
- High contrast and large type throughout. Many users are older or have low vision, and a readable set signals the app is too.
- Keep layouts simple and uncluttered, devices straight-on, captions large with one idea each.
- Soft pill illustrations, and warm caregiver photos (licensed models) for the family shot.
- Leaders use blue/white palettes, a notification as the hero and family avatars. Cold hospital imagery and tiny text cost installs.

## Avoid

- Dosage recommendations or dose calculators. Apple 1.4.2 requires these to come from a manufacturer, hospital, university, insurer or similarly approved entity. Never show "recommended dose".
- Drug interaction warnings unless sourced from a licensed database. They also bring medical-device considerations.
- Real drug brand names and packaging (trademarks). Use generic demo names ("Vitamin D", "Med A") and plain pill icons.
- Absolute promises ("never miss a dose"). Delivery depends on notification permissions and OS behavior, so promise reminders, not outcomes.
- Real patient data, and "HIPAA compliant" or "medical grade" badges unless verified.
- Treatment or health outcome claims ("improves your condition"). Under Apple 1.4.1 and Google Play's health policy, medical claims need evidence.
- Prices, insurance or pharmacy discounts in screenshots. No invented ratings or "{users} patients" counts.
- Conversion killers: tiny text, crowded medication lists, and cold clinical imagery that feels like a hospital form.
