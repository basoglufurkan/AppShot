# Skincare & Beauty

## The searcher

- Someone overwhelmed by products, ingredients and social-media routines who wants a simple routine for their own skin type. They search "skincare routine".
- A young adult focused on breakouts or texture who wants guidance without a dermatologist visit.
- An ingredient-conscious shopper who scans products to check what's inside and what not to mix.
- Someone in their 30s–50s building an anti-aging and SPF habit.
- What they want to feel: "A routine that suits my skin, products that work together, and visible consistency."

## What wins the install

1. **A personalized routine.** "Made for your skin type" after a quiz or scan is the core promise, and it removes overwhelm.
2. **The skin scan as a curiosity hook.** An AI analysis overlay draws attention. Its wording must stay cosmetic and wellness, never diagnostic.
3. **Ingredient clarity.** Scanning a product to see what's inside and spot clashes (retinol plus acids) is concrete, useful and very shareable.
4. **AM/PM structure and reminders.** Step-by-step routines and SPF nudges turn intention into habit.
5. **Honest progress tracking.** A photo diary under consistent light shows change over weeks and builds retention.
6. **Inclusive, aspirational aesthetics.** Real skin texture, varied tones and ages. The audience notices airbrushing.

## Supporting elements

- Routine card: AM / PM tabs with steps and generic bottle illustrations (no brand labels).
- Skin profile card: Type Combination · Concern Texture · Sensitivity Low.
- Ingredient card: "Niacinamide: supports the skin barrier" and a warning chip "Avoid mixing with {ingredient}".
- Scan overlay: soft rings on forehead and cheeks with cosmetic labels (hydration, oiliness, texture).
- Reminder notification: "Reapply sunscreen: it's been {hours} hours outdoors".
- Progress photo timeline (week 1 / week 4) in consistent lighting, with no filters.
- Product shelf grid with "opened {months} months ago" expiry chips.

## Visual direction

- Rose, cream, luxe and pastel palettes: dewy textures, water droplets, soft daylight, marble or linen surfaces, minimal compositions.
- Typography: elegant serif headlines with a clean sans for labels. Beauty-editorial spacing.
- Inclusive models across skin tones, ages and genders, with visible real texture. Airbrushed perfection reads as fake and sets unrealistic expectations.
- Leaders use close-up faces with scan overlays, flatlays of products and routine step cards. Keep product shots unbranded unless you have partnerships.

## Avoid

- Diagnostic claims ("diagnose acne", "detect rosacea", "skin cancer check", "detect melanoma"). That's medical-device territory under Apple 1.4.1 and Google Play's health policy. Scans must be framed as cosmetic or wellness insights.
- Before/after photos that are retouched, filtered or not from real use. They're misleading (Apple 2.3.1), and cosmetic outcome claims are tightly regulated in many markets.
- "Dermatologist approved / recommended" badges without documented endorsement.
- Brand product images or logos without rights (Apple 2.3.9).
- Skin-lightening or whitening framing. It's a colorism and policy issue, so keep to "even tone" or "dark spots" language.
- Aggressive anti-aging messaging aimed at teens.
- Prices, affiliate discounts or "shop now" banners. No invented ratings or "{users} glowing users".
- Conversion killers: only flawless model faces (unrelatable), dense ingredient tables in early shots, and clinical styling that feels like a pharmacy.
