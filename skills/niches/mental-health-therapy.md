# Mental Health & Therapy

## The searcher

- Someone struggling with anxiety, low mood or burnout, often searching late at night, who wants support that feels safe and non-judgmental.
- Someone considering therapy for the first time but held back by cost, stigma, waiting lists or logistics.
- A current or former therapy client who wants CBT/DBT tools between sessions.
- What they want to feel: "Someone understands, this is safe, and I have a next step."

Decide first what the app actually offers: licensed professionals, self-guided evidence-based tools, or an AI companion. Every caption must make that unambiguous.

## What wins the install

1. **Warmth and safety first.** Tone decides installs here. Gentle language, soft visuals and human warmth beat feature claims.
2. **Clarity about the type of support.** Licensed therapists vs self-guided tools vs AI. Ambiguity destroys trust, and in this niche it's a policy and legal risk too.
3. **Evidence-based tools.** Named techniques (CBT thought records, grounding, DBT skills) signal credibility to people who've done some research.
4. **Privacy and confidentiality.** Often the deciding objection, especially for people hiding their search from others.
5. **Access.** From home, on your schedule, text or video. That removes logistical barriers.
6. **Visible crisis resources.** Showing that help exists for the worst moments increases trust. It doesn't scare people away.

## Story (shot by shot)

1. **Hook** — emotional reassurance — show: a warm, simple check-in screen ("How are you feeling today?") with a gentle caption — layout: text-hero — angle: support for hard days.
2. **Support** — what you get — show: a conversation with a licensed therapist (demo names, credential shown) or a guided CBT exercise flow, clearly labeled — layout: chat-bubbles — angle: talk it through, at your pace.

5. **Privacy** — kill the objection — show: confidentiality, encryption and data controls (only true claims) — layout: feature-list — angle: your space, private and safe.
6. **Credibility** — close — show: one real, permissioned user review, or verified credentials ("Licensed in {regions}") — layout: review-quote — angle: care that meets you where you are.

## Supporting elements

- Mood check-in card: a 5-point color scale or soft faces with "How are you today?".
- Thought record card: Situation → Automatic thought → Balanced reframe.
- Chat bubbles with a therapist (avatar, demo name, "Licensed {credential}"), or an AI guide labeled "AI guide, not a therapist".
- Grounding card: "5 things you can see, 4 you can touch…".
- Privacy badges: "Confidential", "Encrypted", "You control your data", only if true.
- Crisis card: "In crisis? Call {localCrisisLine}", with the real number for each locale.
- Mood trend chart over {weeks} weeks with gentle annotations.

## Visual direction

- Calm, cream, sky and pastel palettes: warm, soft, rounded shapes, gentle illustrations or real warm faces (licensed models).
- Typography: humanist sans, sentence case, gentle verbs. Use highlights sparingly. Loud accent colors feel pushy here.
- Avoid both clinical hospital blue-white and dark, sad stock imagery (head in hands, rain on windows). Show the hopeful side.
- Leaders use illustrated characters, warm gradients, therapist profile cards and real attributed quotes. Calm credibility beats energy.

## Avoid

- Diagnosis or cure claims: "cure depression", "diagnose anxiety", "replace therapy", "clinically proven" without published evidence. Apple 1.4.1 and Google Play's health and medical policies apply. Medical claims need evidence and are likely to trigger review.
- Calling an AI chatbot a "therapist" or its chats "therapy". It's misleading, and several US states (for example Illinois) now restrict AI being presented as therapy.
- Licensing or insurance claims that aren't verifiable in every market the screenshots appear in.
- No visible crisis pathway. Reviewers check self-harm handling. Never depict self-harm or suicidal content in screenshots.
- Real patient chats or stories, and fabricated or unpermissioned testimonials.
- Stigmatizing words ("crazy", "fix yourself") and distressing imagery.
- Privacy promises inconsistent with the privacy policy, App Privacy labels or Play Data safety form. Sharing health data with advertisers is an Apple 5.1.3 issue with a well-known enforcement history.
- Prices, session costs or insurance coverage in screenshots.
