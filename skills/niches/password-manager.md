# Password Manager

## The searcher

- Locked out again — just hit "Forgot password" for the third time this month and finally wants a fix.
- Received a breach email ("your data was exposed") and feels exposed and a bit scared.
- Storing passwords in a notes app, a spreadsheet or on sticky notes and knows it's risky.
- A family sharing streaming, Wi-Fi and banking logins; a small team sharing tool accounts.
- After installing they want to feel: never think about passwords again, and genuinely safe.

## What wins the install

1. **Effortless autofill** — the delight moment: tap a login field, glance at the phone, done.
2. **Security you can understand** — "only you hold the key" (zero-knowledge) explained in plain words, not cipher names.
3. **Proactive protection** — breach alerts and a password health score show the app working for you.
4. **Easy switch** — import from browsers and other managers in minutes removes the setup fear.
5. **Modern auth in one place** — passkeys and 2FA codes alongside passwords.
6. **Sharing safely** — family or team vaults without texting passwords.

## Supporting elements

- **Autofill pill**: "Fill with {appName} · {username}" over a generic sign-in page.
- **Health ring**: "Security score {score}/100" from a demo vault.
- **Breach notification**: "A password you use appeared in a breach — change it" (generic site name).
- **OTP tile**: "{code}" with a circular countdown.
- **Generator**: random password + "Strong" meter + length slider.
- **Vault item cards**: Login · Card (masked) · Secure note · Passkey.
- **Comparison**: "Same password everywhere" vs "Unique password for every site".

## Visual direction

- Trust and calm: midnight or finance-pro dark themes, or clean-light for mainstream consumers; one confident blue/teal accent.
- Vault items as crisp floating cards; subtle glass for the "vault" metaphor; locks and shields used sparingly.
- Mono or sharp sans (Inter, IBM Plex) for codes and generated passwords — they should look precise.
- Leaders (1Password, Bitwarden, Dashlane, Proton Pass) show one flow per shot and keep security language human.
- Use generic, fictional websites and usernames; never real brand logos.
- Avoid hacker clichés (green code rain, hooded figures).

## Avoid

- Absolutes: "unhackable", "100% secure", "military-grade" (Apple 2.3.1).
- Scare tactics with fake data: "You've been hacked!", invented breach lists, alarm-red screens — Google Play deceptive-behaviour policy; Apple 2.3.1.
- Real brand logos (Google, Netflix, banks) in vault lists — trademarks; use generic or invented sites.
- Real passwords, emails or card numbers — even in demos.
- Apple's Face ID/Touch ID glyphs or names outside Apple's guidelines; "Keychain" comparisons naming Apple; competitor names in captions (2.3.7).
- Audit or certification badges (SOC 2, ISO 27001, independent audit) unless real and current.
- Conversion mistakes: a lock icon with no product, cipher jargon (AES-256, PBKDF2) on shot 1, the master-password screen as the hook.
